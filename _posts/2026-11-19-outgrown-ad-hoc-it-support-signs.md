Most small businesses start the same way. Someone's cousin sets up the laptops, a freelancer is called when the printer fails, or the most computer-literate member of staff becomes the unofficial IT person. For a team of five, this works. For a team of thirty, with customer data, several offices and software the business cannot run without, it quietly becomes a risk.

This is **ad hoc IT support**: help that is arranged only when something breaks, with no agreement, no records and no one responsible for the whole picture. Below are the signs that it is time to move to an **IT support company in Lagos** (or wherever you operate) that works to a written agreement.

## Sign 1: Problems are fixed, but they keep coming back

If the same slow Wi-Fi, full server drive or failing email keeps returning every few weeks, nobody is looking for the root cause. Ad hoc support is paid to fix the symptom and leave. A provider with ongoing responsibility has a reason to find out why it happens.

## Sign 2: Only one person knows how things work

Ask yourself: if your freelancer or IT officer left tomorrow, could someone else find the router password, the licence keys, the backup schedule and the domain registration details? If the answer is "only he or she knows", your business has a single point of failure that no UPS can protect against.

Good IT support keeps **documentation**, meaning a written record of your network, systems, accounts and suppliers, that belongs to you.

## Sign 3: You find out about problems from users

Staff tell you the server is down, or that they cannot send email, before anyone in IT knows. **Proactive IT monitoring** reverses this: software watches your equipment and raises an alert about a failing disk, a full drive or a dropping internet link so that it is dealt with before work stops. Platforms such as ManageEngine are examples of tools used for this. Ad hoc arrangements rarely include it.

## Sign 4: Nobody can say when the last backup was tested

Having a backup is not the same as being able to restore from it. If nobody has restored a file or a server from backup in the last few months, you do not know whether it works. Our guide to the [3-2-1 backup rule](/blog/3-2-1-backup-rule-for-business/) explains how to set this up properly, and someone must be accountable for checking it.

## Sign 5: Updates and security basics are patchy

Warning signs include laptops that have not been updated for months, no consistent antivirus or endpoint protection, former staff whose accounts are still active, and no multi-factor authentication on email. These are the gaps attackers rely on. They are also the sort of tasks that get postponed when support only happens in a crisis.

## Sign 6: Downtime is costing real money

Add up what an hour without email, internet or your accounting system costs. For a retailer, a bank branch or a clinic, it can be lost sales, delayed customers or staff paid to wait. If one day of downtime would cost more than a month of proper support, the economics have already shifted.

## Sign 7: You have grown and the IT has not

Typical triggers are a second branch, remote staff, a move to Microsoft 365 or cloud systems, a new ERP, or a customer or regulator asking how you protect data. Under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA), organisations that handle personal data are expected to have appropriate security measures. That is difficult to demonstrate when IT is a series of favours.

## Sign 8: You have no idea what you own

Unknown laptop ages, software licences that no one can find, and warranties that expire unnoticed all lead to surprise bills and unlicensed software. An **asset register**, a simple list of devices, owners, purchase dates and renewal dates, is a standard part of managed support.

## A quick self-check

Count how many of these are true for your business:

- We do not have a written agreement with whoever supports our IT.
- There is no ticket or log of issues and how they were resolved.
- We wait hours or days for a response when something fails.
- Passwords and admin access are held by one person.
- We have never tested restoring from backup.
- We do not know which laptops and licences we own.
- A power or internet failure at a branch means someone has to travel to fix it.
- We have no IT budget, only emergency spending.

Two or three ticks suggest you should tighten up. Five or more mean you are very likely carrying avoidable risk.

## What to look for in a provider

Moving up does not have to mean a large contract. Look for:

1. A written scope and a **service level agreement (SLA)** with defined response times.
2. A helpdesk with ticketing, so every request is recorded.
3. Remote support for quick fixes, with on-site visits for what cannot be done remotely.
4. Monitoring, patching and backup checks included, not sold only as extras.
5. Documentation and admin credentials handed to you, not kept by the provider.
6. Regular reviews and a clear annual plan.

## Making the move

You do not have to replace everyone at once. Many businesses keep a trusted IT officer and add a provider for monitoring, the helpdesk and specialist work. A sensible first step is a short review of your current environment, which produces a list of risks, an asset register and a priority plan.
