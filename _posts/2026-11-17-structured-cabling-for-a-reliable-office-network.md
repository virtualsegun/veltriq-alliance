When an office network misbehaves, the usual suspects are the router, the Wi-Fi or the internet provider. Often the real culprit is far less glamorous: the cables behind the walls, in the ceiling and in the cabinet. Loose connectors, over-long runs, cables bundled with power lines and a tangle of unlabelled patch leads cause drops, slow speeds and faults that are maddening to trace.

**Structured cabling** is the cure. It is a planned, standards-based cabling system that serves your whole building, instead of cables added one at a time whenever someone needed a socket. For any business planning an office network setup or a fit-out, structured cabling in Lagos is one of the best investments you can make, because it is installed once and should serve you for many years.

## What structured cabling actually is

Structured cabling is an organised system of cables, sockets, panels and cabinets built to a published design. Its main parts are:

- **Work-area outlets.** The wall or floor sockets where PCs, phones, printers, access points and cameras connect.
- **Horizontal cabling.** The cables that run from each outlet back to a communications cabinet on the same floor. For copper, this is usually twisted-pair cable (commonly called Cat6 or Cat6A) and each run has a maximum length of 90 metres.
- **Patch panels.** Rows of numbered sockets in the cabinet where every cable ends. Short patch leads link the panel to the network switch.
- **Communications cabinets (racks).** Enclosed frames that hold the panels, switches and other equipment in one tidy, lockable place.
- **Backbone cabling.** Links between floors or buildings, often optical fibre, because fibre carries more data over longer distances and is not affected by electrical interference.

Because everything is numbered and documented, you can move a person from one desk to another by changing a patch lead at the cabinet instead of pulling new cable.

## Why cabling quality matters for performance

Wi-Fi gets the attention, but every access point, IP phone and CCTV camera still depends on a cable. Poor cabling shows up as:

- Speeds far below what the switch and internet link should deliver.
- Intermittent drops that disappear when someone wiggles a connector.
- IP cameras and phones that reboot at random, especially where they are powered through the network cable.
- Faults that are hard to diagnose because nobody knows where a cable goes.

Good cabling is also the best preparation for future upgrades. A building cabled properly today can usually move to faster switches and newer Wi-Fi without opening the ceiling again.

## Choosing cable: copper, fibre and what you need

For most offices, the practical choices are straightforward:

1. **Cat6 copper** suits most desktop and phone connections.
2. **Cat6A copper** costs more but handles higher speeds over the full 90 metres, and is worth considering for access points and heavy-use areas.
3. **Optical fibre** is the sensible choice between floors, buildings and server rooms.

Ask for cable that is certified to the standard, not just labelled with it. Cheap copper-clad aluminium cable is sold in some markets as network cable. It performs poorly, heats up when carrying power to devices and is a false economy. Your installer should specify genuine solid copper cable from a recognised manufacturer.

## What good installation looks like

The quality of the installation matters as much as the cable. A well-run job includes:

- **A site survey and design first.** The installer walks the building, counts the outlets you need today, and plans extra capacity for growth.
- **Separation from power cables.** Network cables should not run alongside mains cables for long distances, as electrical interference degrades the signal.
- **Correct bend and pull handling.** Cables that are pulled too hard or bent too sharply can fail tests or fail later.
- **Neat termination.** Every connector is punched down properly, with no more untwisting of pairs than the standard allows.
- **Labels at both ends.** Every cable, outlet and panel port carries a matching label.
- **Testing and a report.** Each run is tested with a certification tester, and you receive the results.
- **Documentation.** Floor plans and a cable schedule, so the next engineer is not guessing.

## Planning for Nigerian conditions

A few local realities deserve attention when you plan:

- **Power and earthing.** Poor earthing and unstable supply can damage network equipment. Cabinets should be properly earthed and equipment protected by a suitable UPS (uninterruptible power supply, a battery unit that keeps equipment running through short cuts).
- **Heat.** Cabinets in poorly ventilated cupboards cook equipment. Allow for airflow or cooling, as described in our guide to [keeping your server room running through power cuts and heat](/blog/keeping-your-server-room-running-through-power-cuts-and-heat/).
- **Pests and moisture.** In ceilings and ducts, use trunking or conduit where appropriate to protect cable.
- **Growth.** Offices rarely shrink. Plan more outlets than you need now, including ceiling points for Wi-Fi access points and cameras.

## A cabling checklist before you sign

Before you approve a quotation, ask:

- Which cable category and brand will be used, and is it solid copper?
- How many outlets, and how many spare ports in the cabinet?
- Will every run be tested and the results handed over?
- Will cables and ports be labelled, and will you get drawings?
- Who is responsible for fixing faults after handover, and for how long?
- Is the cabinet earthed, ventilated and lockable?

If a quote gives only a price per point with no detail on these items, expect corners to be cut.

## Cabling is the start of a network, not the whole of it

Cabling does not replace sensible [network design](/blog/5-signs-your-business-network-needs-an-upgrade/). It is the base on which switches, Wi-Fi, firewalls and internet redundancy sit. Get it right and the rest of the network has a chance to perform; get it wrong and no expensive equipment will fix it.
