Most offices buy Wi-Fi the same way: count the floors, buy one access point per floor or per few rooms, mount them where a cable happens to end, and hope. Then the complaints start. The boardroom drops video calls, the reception area is fine, and the far end of the open-plan floor "has no signal". Staff blame the internet provider, who is often not at fault.

A **wireless site survey** is the step that prevents this. It is an assessment of your building, done before or during installation, that shows where Wi-Fi signal will reach, where it will be weak, and how many access points you really need. This guide explains what a survey measures, why buildings in Lagos can be tricky, and how to plan enterprise Wi-Fi in Nigeria without overspending.

## What a wireless site survey is

An **access point** is the device on a ceiling or wall that broadcasts Wi-Fi. A site survey works out how many you need and exactly where they should go. There are three common types:

- **Predictive survey.** Specialist software models your floor plan, wall materials and expected users, and suggests access point positions. It is done from drawings, often before you move in.
- **On-site (active) survey.** An engineer walks the real building with measuring equipment and test access points, recording signal strength and interference room by room.
- **Post-installation validation.** After installation, the engineer measures again to confirm that the coverage matches the plan, and adjusts if not.

For a new fit-out, a predictive survey followed by validation is a sensible combination. For an existing office with complaints, an on-site survey is more useful because it shows what is actually happening.

## What the survey measures

Good surveys go beyond "how many bars do I have?". They look at:

- **Signal strength.** Whether the signal at every desk and meeting room is strong enough for reliable use, not only for a quick test.
- **Interference.** Other Wi-Fi networks, cordless equipment and electronic devices competing for the same radio channels. In dense Lagos business districts, neighbouring offices can fill the airwaves.
- **Capacity.** How many people and devices will connect at once. A room of 40 people with a laptop and a phone each is a very different load from 40 people with only a phone.
- **Roaming.** Whether a phone or laptop moves smoothly from one access point to the next as someone walks along a corridor, so a video call does not freeze.
- **Dead zones.** Stairwells, lift lobbies, store rooms and corners where coverage fails.

## Why buildings in Lagos can be difficult

Wi-Fi signals are weakened by what they pass through. Thick concrete walls, steel reinforcement, glass with metallic coating, lift shafts and server room doors all absorb or reflect signal. Older buildings with solid block walls can make a single access point cover far less than the brochure promises.

Other local factors worth raising with your installer:

- **Power.** Access points are usually powered through the network cable (called **Power over Ethernet, or PoE**) from a switch. If that switch is on a UPS, your Wi-Fi survives a short power cut, which matters when generator changeover takes a few minutes.
- **Heat.** Ceiling voids can get hot. Equipment and mounting locations should suit the temperature.
- **Neighbouring networks.** Shared buildings often mean many overlapping networks, so channel planning matters more than raw signal strength.
- **Visitors.** Clients, contractors and staff phones all want access, which affects capacity and security design.

## The pre-survey checklist

Before a surveyor visits, gather:

1. **Floor plans** for every floor, even rough drawings with room names.
2. **Number of staff** per area, and how many devices each person typically carries.
3. **What the Wi-Fi must do.** Email and browsing, video calls, cloud applications, handheld scanners, point-of-sale terminals, IP cameras, or guest access.
4. **Special areas.** Boardrooms, training rooms, canteens, warehouses and outdoor spaces such as car parks and gardens.
5. **Cable routes.** Where network cabling already exists or can be added. Good [structured cabling](/blog/structured-cabling-for-a-reliable-office-network/) decides where access points can sit.
6. **Growth plans.** Staff you expect to add in the next two to three years.

## What you should receive afterwards

A professional survey should end with a written report, not just a verbal opinion. Look for:

- A floor plan marked with recommended access point positions and mounting heights.
- Coverage maps showing signal strength across each floor.
- A list of equipment, including access points, switches with PoE capacity and cabling needs.
- Channel and security recommendations, including a separate guest network.
- Notes on anything that will need fixing in the building, such as missing cable routes or ceiling access.

Keep the report. It makes future changes cheaper, because the next engineer does not have to start from nothing.

## Enterprise Wi-Fi versus home-style routers

A home router can look like a bargain, but offices outgrow it quickly. **Enterprise Wi-Fi** systems from vendors such as Aruba, Cisco, Huawei and Ruijie are designed for many users at once. They allow central management of every access point from one screen, automatic channel selection, support for separate staff and guest networks, and reporting when something goes wrong. Buying cheaper devices is not always a saving if you need to replace them after a year, or if nobody can see why the network is slow.

## Mistakes a survey helps you avoid

- **Too few access points.** The most common cause of slow Wi-Fi.
- **No guest separation.** Visitors on the same network as your finance systems.
