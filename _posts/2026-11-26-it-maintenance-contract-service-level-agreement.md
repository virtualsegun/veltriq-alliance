Many disputes with IT suppliers start the same way. The business assumed the provider would fix everything; the provider assumed it was only responsible for what was written down. When the server fails on a Monday morning, nobody can point to a page that says who does what, and by when.

A well-written **IT maintenance contract**, with a clear service level agreement attached, removes that guesswork. This guide explains what each part should contain, in plain language, so that you can draft one or review a supplier's proposal with confidence. This is general guidance, not legal advice; have a lawyer review the final text.

## The contract and the SLA: what is the difference?

The **contract** covers the commercial relationship: who the parties are, the fees, the length of the agreement, liability and how either side can end it.

The **service level agreement (SLA)** is the part that describes the service itself in measurable terms: how quickly the provider responds, how quickly problems are resolved, and what happens when targets are missed. Most businesses attach the SLA to the contract as a schedule so that it can be updated without rewriting everything.

## Start with the scope of work

The scope is the most important section. Write down, in a list, what is covered:

- Which sites, offices and branches are included.
- Which equipment: servers, laptops, desktops, printers, switches, Wi-Fi access points, firewalls, CCTV and phones.
- Which software and services: Microsoft 365 administration, antivirus or endpoint protection, backup, email filtering.
- Which activities: helpdesk support, monitoring, patching (installing software security updates), backup checks and user account changes.
- How many users are covered, and how new joiners are added.

Attach an **asset register**, a list of devices with serial numbers and purchase dates, so there is no argument later about what was included. Our guide on [signs you have outgrown ad hoc IT support](/blog/outgrown-ad-hoc-it-support-signs/) explains why.

## Be explicit about exclusions

Equally useful is what is not covered. Common exclusions include new projects such as moving offices, hardware purchases and replacement, repairs to equipment out of warranty, and damage caused by power surges or misuse. If you want these included, say so and price them. If they are excluded, agree how they will be quoted, for example a daily or hourly rate.

## Define response and resolution times

This is the heart of the SLA. Two terms are often confused:

- **Response time** is how long before a qualified person starts working on your ticket.
- **Resolution time** is how long before the problem is fixed or a workaround is in place.

Set targets by priority level. An example structure, which you should adapt to your own business:

| Priority | Example | Response target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Whole office offline, server down | Within an hour or less |
| High | One department cannot work | A few hours |
| Normal | Single user problem | Same or next business day |
| Low | Request for new software or advice | Within agreed days |

Targets must be realistic for the fee: a 30-minute response around the clock costs far more than a four-hour response in office hours. State the **support hours** clearly, including whether weekends and public holidays are covered.

## Reporting, monitoring and on-site visits

Specify the channels (phone, email, a ticket portal) and who may log requests. Every request should generate a **ticket** with a reference number, which gives you a record and makes monthly reports possible. If the provider practises **proactive IT monitoring**, name what is watched (servers, internet links, backups, disk space).

Most faults can be fixed remotely, but not a failed switch or a cut cable. State how many on-site visits are included, how quickly an engineer attends critical faults and whether call-out charges apply, especially for branches outside Lagos.

## Backups, security and data protection

If the provider manages your backups, the contract should say what is backed up, how often, where copies are kept and how often a restore is tested. See our guide to [the 3-2-1 backup rule](/blog/3-2-1-backup-rule-for-business/) for the standard to aim for.

Because the provider will have access to your systems and possibly personal data, include confidentiality terms and a data processing clause. Under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA), an organisation that uses another company to handle personal data on its behalf remains responsible for protecting it, so the contract should require appropriate security measures and prompt notice of any breach. Confirm the specific obligations with your legal adviser and, where needed, the Nigeria Data Protection Commission's guidance.

## Fees, payment and price changes

Spell out the monthly or annual fee, what it covers, how extra work is charged, and in which currency. If any part of the cost depends on imported hardware or licences priced in US dollars, say how naira exchange-rate movements will be handled, rather than leaving it to a surprise invoice. Note when the fee can be reviewed and what notice you will receive.

## Service credits and what happens when targets are missed

Many SLAs include **service credits**, a discount on the next invoice when the provider misses its targets. Also agree what counts as repeated failure and gives you the right to end the contract early.

## Term, exit and handover

Check the minimum term, the notice period and any renewal that happens automatically. Most important is the exit clause. It should say that you own your documentation and that the provider must hand over administrator passwords, licence details and network diagrams promptly if the relationship ends. Without this, changing supplier can be painful.

## A review checklist

Before you sign, confirm that the document lists what is covered and excluded, gives response targets by priority with support hours, explains ticketing and monthly reporting, sets backup and data protection duties, states fees and price review rules, and defines termination and handover of credentials and records.

## Keep it alive

Review the agreement with your provider at least twice a year, and update the scope when you open a branch, adopt Microsoft 365 or add staff. For a wider view of what to expect, read [what managed IT services include and how they are priced](/blog/managed-it-services-nigeria-what-they-include/).
