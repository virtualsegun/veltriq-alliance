Most cyber attacks on businesses do not begin with clever hacking. They begin with an email that looks ordinary: a supplier invoice, a bank alert, a request from the managing director, a "shared document" from a colleague. One member of staff clicks, types a password or opens an attachment, and the attacker is inside.

This is **phishing**: a message designed to trick someone into handing over credentials, money or access. Knowing how to spot a phishing email is one of the cheapest and most effective defences a business has, and it does not require technical knowledge. This guide gives your staff a checklist they can use every day.

## Why phishing works so well in Nigerian businesses

Phishing succeeds because it targets people and routines rather than software. Several features of daily working life make it easier:

- **Heavy use of email and WhatsApp for approvals.** Payment instructions and supplier details are often agreed informally, so a forged request does not look out of place.
- **Pressure and hierarchy.** Staff are reluctant to question a message that appears to come from a director or a bank.
- **Mobile-first reading.** On a phone, the full sender address and link destination are hidden, which makes fakes harder to spot.
- **Familiar brands.** Attackers copy banks, courier firms, government agencies and Microsoft sign-in pages convincingly.

## The phishing checklist

Ask these questions before clicking, replying or paying.

### 1. Is the sender really who they claim to be?

- Look at the full email address, not just the display name. "Accounts Department" can be sent from any address at all.
- Watch for lookalike domains: a swapped letter, an extra word, or a different ending such as `.co` instead of `.com`.
- Be wary if a known contact suddenly writes from a personal or unfamiliar address.

### 2. Is it creating urgency or fear?

Phrases such as "your account will be suspended today", "act immediately" or "payment overdue" are designed to stop you thinking. Real organisations rarely demand that you act within minutes. If a message makes you anxious, pause.

### 3. Does it ask for something unusual?

Treat these as red flags, whoever appears to be asking:

- Login details, one-time codes or PINs. No genuine bank or IT team will ask for these by email.
- A change to a supplier's bank account details.
- Gift cards, urgent transfers or "keep this confidential" instructions.
- A request that bypasses your normal approval process.

### 4. Where does the link really go?

- On a computer, hover over the link without clicking and read the address shown in the corner of the window.
- On a phone, press and hold the link to preview it.
- Be suspicious of shortened links and of addresses that contain the real brand name only as part of a longer, different address.
- When in doubt, do not use the link. Type the website address yourself or use a saved bookmark.

### 5. Is the attachment expected?

Be careful with unexpected invoices, "scanned documents", voice messages and zip files. Files that ask you to "enable macros" or "enable content" are a classic route for malware. If you were not expecting it, confirm with the sender using another channel.

### 6. Does the message look slightly off?

Generic greetings such as "Dear customer", odd wording, poor formatting and mismatched logos are warning signs, though modern phishing is often well written. Do not rely on spelling mistakes alone. A polished message can still be fake.

## Verify through a second channel

The single most useful habit is simple: if a message asks for money, data or a password, check it by another route. Call the person on a number you already hold, not one given in the email. For payment changes, agree a rule that nobody updates supplier bank details without a call-back to a known contact. ## What to do if someone clicks

Mistakes happen. What matters is how quickly they are reported. Make sure staff know to:

1. **Report it straight away** to IT or the person responsible, without fear of blame. Delay is what turns a small incident into a large one.
2. **Change the password** of any account they typed it into, from a different, trusted device, and on any other site where they reuse it.
3. **Disconnect** the device from the network if they opened a suspicious attachment, and not switch it off until IT advises.
4. **Keep the email** rather than deleting it, so it can be investigated and other staff warned.

## Technical controls that reduce the risk

Staff awareness works best alongside technology that stops many phishing emails from arriving at all, and limits the damage when one gets through:

- **Email security filtering** to block known malicious messages, links and attachments. Microsoft 365 includes protection features, and products from vendors such as Sophos add further layers.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**, which asks for a second proof of identity, such as a code from an app, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
- **Email authentication records** (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) that make it harder for attackers to send messages pretending to be from your own domain.
- **Endpoint protection** on laptops and phones to catch malware if an attachment is opened.
- **Regular awareness training** and simulated phishing exercises, which platforms such as KnowBe4 are built to deliver.

## A short checklist to share

- Check the sender's real address.
- Stop and think if the message is urgent or threatening.
- Never share passwords or one-time codes.
- Hover over links before clicking.
- Confirm payment or account changes by phone.
- Report anything suspicious, even if you already clicked.

Print it, pin it near the finance desk, and repeat it in staff meetings. Habits form through repetition.
